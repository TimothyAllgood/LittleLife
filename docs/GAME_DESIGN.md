# Little Life — game design and editorial direction

## Fantasy
Live a different life every run. Small choices can become legends at family reunions; big plans can fail. The player is allowed to be kind, selfish, reckless, responsible, lucky, unlucky, awkward, or occasionally astonishing.

## Core loop
Make choices and spend activity opportunities → see named concrete outcomes → age up to a new life chapter → respond to events shaped by relationships, skills, finances and past decisions → optionally continue as the next generation.

## Life stages
- **Infant/toddler:** first words/steps, caretakers, siblings, daycare, pets, curiosity. The child does not magically earn a salary.
- **Child:** school personalities, clubs, hobbies, minor jobs/allowances, neighbors, small risks, best friends, rivalries and family tensions.
- **Teen:** exams, friendships, popularity, cliques, romances, rivalries, social media embarrassment, driving, jobs, fights and accountability.
- **Young adult:** study, careers, relationships, moving, budgets, pets, travel, creative ambitions, rooming with strangers and starting families.
- **Adult:** work, family generations, raising children, caring for relatives, housing, financial setbacks, relationships and long-term decisions.
- **Senior:** health, memories, rekindled relationships, retirement, mentoring, legacy and difficult goodbyes.

## Tone and rating
Target roughly PG-13. The game can be occasionally sad, chaotic and morbid in a non-graphic way. Violence can cause injuries, legal trouble and strain. Deliberate cruelty shouldn't automatically work or grant popularity. Do not provide instructions to commit crime or frame explicit content as entertainment.

## Narrative standard
A character should not just 'paint a picture' but paint *a crooked lighthouse in a thunderstorm* and see whether it gets placed in a café or used to cover a hole in the wall. Friends have names, flaws and memories. Branches must be different in kind, not rephrased '+5 happiness'. Setups avoid filler like 'something exciting happens', 'a surprising opportunity' and 'you had a great time'.

## Ordinary, unusual, impossible
Ordinary episodes are the backbone of the game; weird news and strange mysteries are the spice. Rare storylines should require eligibility and stay very unlikely. A player might never see aliens, temporal anomalies or cryptids across several lives. Multi-step arcs should avoid contradicting a save's family and social facts.

## Relationships, alignment and stats
Skill checks change likelihood of outcomes, not just progress bars. Alignment records patterns of choices but does not turn into a simplistic 'evil = automatic failure' rule. Popularity is a volatile social measure, separate from whether one real friend trusts you. NPCs can become rivals, stay angry and change roles as time passes.

## Avoiding sameness
- Give each activity selectable subtypes, tools and projects.
- Add cooldowns and life-history gating to stories that should happen only once.
- Favor callbacks to choices made years earlier over isolated achievements.
- Playtest multiple lives from infant through senior; a great age-17 experience does not fix an empty age-7 experience.

## Backlog ideas
Deeper interactive skill-based mini-stories; authored recurring rivals and families; project and instrument equipment; more custom DiceBear variation controls mapped to exact style variants; optional local portrait generation; true episodic story editor; code/data separation and more automated outcome assertions.

## Later-life expansion (October 2026)

Teen and adult years should be capable of wonderful, awful, dull, hilarious and strange outcomes. The player may study hard, trick a rival, take the blame for a friend, start a disastrous side business, care for an ill relative, get fired, or begin a surprise second career. Senior years include choosing retirement, changing living arrangements, friendships and rivalries in retirement communities, projects, community events and occasional bizarre mishaps. Loss and illness should be present but never graphically depicted or mocked.

The post-childhood catalog (`assets/later-life.js`) adds 80 everyday/conditional stories and five multi-year arcs. Consequences include finances, grades, legal trouble, relationship bonds, skills, health, reputation, popularity and future callbacks. The old event library remains available and should not become invisible beneath the new material. Player activities include named skill showcases with prizes/trophies, actual choices during work shifts, sleepovers and healthcare. Random success rates are affected by relevant skills and circumstances, not just a coin toss.

The Journal and Home show a state-based life chapter, not a static instructions panel. These recaps are written from current family relationships, career, town, recent events and health; events should be memorable enough for the recap to say something specific. Prior character generations are kept in the existing family tree and history.

## Background life, accomplishments, and work

A year should not feel empty just because the player didn't click on anything. Ambient stories should be a mix of mundane, cruel, chaotic, lucky and unexpectedly touching; 2–3 small entries at most per ordinary year keeps the journal readable. Major losses and births should be grounded in actual character records. Earlier promises can return later with specific recollections and titles.

A high skill should unlock distinct opportunities and raise the chance of succeeding at job crises. Practicing may bring mishaps, surprising compliments, minor awards or local notice, not merely another skill number. Employment includes 2+ role-specific situations per career, warnings, dismissal, asking for promotion, and quitting with different social consequences. Stay PG-13 and avoid sensationalizing serious illness or bereavement.

## Activities worth spending a year on

Picking an instrument or rolling up a D&D character is *setup*. Playing a song, writing one, performing, or running a D&D session is what costs a turn and produces a story. Make every paid turn leave evidence: a changed skill, a work in the portfolio, a new quest in the campaign, a trophy, a friend who remembers, a bill for a broken prop, a bruise, or the first step toward a career.

The expanded hobby book adds fencing, historical stage dueling, archaeology, birdwatching, magic, pottery, woodworking, sewing, languages, hiking, geocaching, robotics, fishing, horse riding, calligraphy, stand-up comedy, sailing, model making, debate and fossil hunting. New activity prompts should name **what the character actually does**, not just "practice." The game shows the tangible result and before/after skill scores, including failed attempts. Skill level changes the probability of success, rewards, rare mastery recognition, career eligibility and occasional opportunities. Expert players can still have humiliatingly bad days. Neglected skills can get rusty.

Avoid low-value content like "you practiced and got better" or "your choices affect your future". Prefer "Your homemade robot sorts every sock into a single pile labeled ERROR" or "You find an old railway station platform underneath the blackberries." Outcomes should be specific and, when appropriate, funny, not written as software status messages. Keep stages, positive and negative outcomes, alternative branches, age gates, and lasting callbacks in balance. A hobby should also give the player a reason to return to it after reaching the mastery threshold.
